# Voice Training Methodology

Coaching manual for the voice-trainer agent. Load at the start of every session. Citations reference the
archived evidence base in `med-db/` (topic `voice-feminization`).

## 1. Anatomy in One Paragraph

Voice production = source + filter (source-filter theory, Fant 1960). **Source:** the vocal folds in the
larynx vibrate, setting the fundamental frequency (F0) — perceived as pitch. Higher F0 comes from longer,
thinner, more tensed folds. **Filter:** the vocal tract (throat, mouth, lips, tongue position) shapes the
sound's spectrum — its formant frequencies. Formants are perceived as vocal size (lower formants = larger
perceived body) and brightness (higher formants = brighter). **Vocal weight:** how firmly the folds close —
heavy/buzzy vs. light/soft closure (breathy onset at one extreme, pressed at the other). **Respiration:**
breath support from the diaphragm powers sustained, relaxed phonation. Estrogen does not change the adult
larynx (WPATH SOC-8, PMID 36238954) — voice change comes from training and/or surgery.

## 2. The Four Perceptual Dimensions

Feminization work targets four dimensions (after ASHA Practice Portal "Gender Affirming Voice and
Communication" and the Leyns program structure, PMID 39465090):

1. **Pitch (F0).** The most-studied dimension. Average F0 correlates r = .89 with femininity ratings
   (Wolfe et al. 1990, PMID 2299839); the lowest average F0 identified as female in that study was 155 Hz.
   Common target range: 165–220 Hz (Hawley et al. 2024 used 170–220 Hz, PMID 34706847). Decisive on its
   own, yet insufficient alone (see dimension interplay below).
2. **Resonance.** Formant shaping via tongue position (forward), lip spreading, and larynx height.
   Perceived as brightness/smallness. Hillenbrand & Clark 2009 (PMID 19525544): shifting F0 and formants
   together changed perceived speaker sex ~82% of the time; either alone usually failed. Gelfer & Mikos
   2005 (PMID 16301101): listeners followed F0 even when it contradicted formants. Coaching consequence:
   pitch sets the frame, resonance completes it — train both.
3. **Vocal weight.** Lighter closure reads as more feminine. Trained through breathy-voice onsets,
   aspirated onsets, and SOVTE-based efficiency work — not by pushing air harder (which strains).
4. **Vocal size.** Perceived body size of the voice; reduced through raised larynx, smaller mouth
   resonance, and tongue-forward postures. Often the most intuitive entry point ("smaller" vs "larger"
   sound contrasts).

Supporting dimensions: **intonation** (Wolfe et al. 1990: upward intonation patterns and fewer level
contours distinguished female-categorized trans speakers), **articulation** (precision, forward placement),
**loudness**, **speech rate**, and **nonverbal communication** (ASHA: communicative style is part of
gender-affirming voice work). Dimensions interact — the evidence is clear that multi-dimensional programs
outperform pitch-only work (Leyns et al., PMID 39465090: PET + ART combination > single dimension).

## 3. Assessment Without Hearing

You cannot hear the client. Your assessment instruments are:

- **Self-report of sensations.** The client's perception is the primary data: where they feel vibration
  (chest vs. face/mask), effort level (1–10), strain/pain presence, breath control, resonance "color."
- **Pitch-app readings.** Client measures F0 with a pitch app (e.g., Voice Tools) or tuner: sustained
  vowel (e.g., /a/ at comfortable pitch), reading a short passage, and 30 seconds of spontaneous speech.
  Record all three — the gap between sustained-vowel and spontaneous F0 is itself diagnostic (a large
  gap = skills not yet generalized). Client has no app → recommend free options; readings are a tool,
  not a requirement.
- **Functional measures the client can self-administer.** Maximum phonation time (MPT: sustained /s/
  or /z/ seconds — a rough breath-support index; note the S/Z ratio: markedly different /s/ and /z/
  times can hint at fold closure issues, which is a referral signal, not a diagnosis).
- **TWVQ as a conversation frame.** The Trans Woman Voice Questionnaire (Dacakis et al. 2013,
  PMID 23415146) — 30 items on voice-related quality of life. Use selected items conversationally
  ("How often does your voice keep you from doing what you want?"); formal administration belongs to
  clinical settings. A self-reported TWVQ-style check every few weeks tracks dysphoria reduction, which
  matters independently of Hz (F0 change does not correlate with quality-of-life change — Hao et al.;
  the client's felt experience is its own outcome).
- **Baseline profile.** First session establishes: comfortable F0 (sustained vowel), highest
  strain-free F0, spontaneous-speech F0, voice-use demands (from intake 1i), and the client's own
  description of what they want to change.

Every measurement is client-reported. Never fabricate a reading; never imply you heard something.
If data is missing, ask for it.

## 4. Program Design

Evidence base: structured multi-week programs with daily home practice are the standard (WPATH SOC-8;
Schwarz 2023, PMID 37481572; Leyns et al. RCTs, PMID 39465090; Hawley et al. 2024, PMID 34706847).

- **Duration:** 8–12 weeks as the typical evidence-based frame; extend based on goals and adherence.
  Leyns et al. used 14 weeks with blocks; gains largely stable at 1 year (PMID 38704279).
- **Cadence:** weekly coached session + daily home practice.
- **Home practice dose:** 10–15 minutes daily, in short bouts (e.g., 3 × 5 min). Consistency beats
  marathon sessions; over-practice causes strain. Encourage anchoring practice to an existing habit
  (after brushing teeth, during commute).
- **Structure per session:** warm-up (SOVTE) → review home practice → one new skill → integrate
  (apply new skill into words/reading/spontaneous speech) → assign home practice → brief voice rest
  check.
- **Progression hierarchy — every skill climbs this ladder:** sustained vowel → syllables → words →
  phrases/reading → spontaneous speech. Generalization to spontaneous speech is the goal of all work;
  skills that live only on sustained vowels are not yet functional. The Leyns protocol's core rule:
  pitch target is "as high as possible without strain," never a forced number.
- **Anchor pitches:** work toward a speaking range around 165–220 Hz depending on client anatomy and
  goals (Hawley et al. 2024 target band 170–220 Hz). 220 Hz is a useful training anchor (A3) for
  sustain practice. Some clients — smaller larynxes — manage higher comfortably; larger larynxes may
  need staged targets. Strain-free ceiling is personal; find it, don't impose it.
- **Plateau response:** when progress stalls — vary the dimension (add resonance if pitch-only stalls),
  raise ecological validity (more spontaneous-speech integration), check adherence honestly, and check
  for strain (clients sometimes hold tension that caps the comfortable ceiling).

## 5. Technique Catalog

### 5.1 Semi-Occluded Vocal Tract Exercises (SOVTE) — Warm-Up and Efficiency

- **Straw phonation** (phonating through a drinking straw into water or air): the workhorse warm-up.
  2–3 minutes at start of practice. Glides up and down through comfortable range. Builds balanced
  pressure, reduces strain, improves fold closure efficiency.
- **Cup bubbles** (straw into water bottle): visual feedback via bubbles; steadier bubbles = steadier
  airflow.
- **Lip trills / raspberries:** occlusion without tools; combine with glides.
- **Humming:** gentle onset work; feel vibration in the mask (face) rather than the chest.
- **Progression:** SOVTE warm-up → SOVTE with pitch glides → carry the eased, forward sensation into
  vowels immediately after.

### 5.2 Pitch (F0)

- **Glides:** slide up from comfortable pitch to the strain-free ceiling and back ("siren"), on /a/,
  /o/, or lip trills. Teaches range and flexibility; the ceiling is where the client's personal
  "as high as possible without strain" lives.
- **Sustained vowels:** hold /a/ (or /i/) at a target pitch (e.g., 220 Hz anchor) 5–10 seconds,
  checking strain each time. Client reports app readings.
- **Messa di voce:** crescendo-decrescendo on one pitch — dynamic control without pitch change.
- **Pitch-matching via app:** client watches the app and holds a target band (e.g., 200 ± 10 Hz).
- **Progression:** vowel sustains → count 1–10 on one pitch → carrier phrases ("Good morning!") →
  reading aloud at target pitch → spontaneous speech with periodic app spot-checks.
- **Safety:** never into falsetto as a goal; if the voice flips to falsetto, lower the target. No
  forced shouting of high pitches.

### 5.3 Resonance (Formants) and Vocal Size

- **Forward focus / oral brightness:** hum → "mmm" → open to "m-ah" while keeping the buzz in the
  front of the face. Contrast chest-resonant "dark" production vs. face-resonant "bright" production —
  the client can feel both and choose the bright one.
- **Tongue forward:** speak with tongue tip anchored near the front lower teeth; contrast back-tongue
  ("uh") vs. forward-tongue ("ee") — forward reads smaller/brighter.
- **Lip spreading:** smile-posture vowels; corners forward rather than pursed (with the caution:
  not a rigid smile — spread, not tense).
- **Larynx height:** "uhhh" (low larynx, large resonance) ↔ "eee" (raised larynx, small resonance)
  alternation; then carry the raised-larynx feel into speech. Teach via the silent swallow-and-hold
  or the "ee" glide — never forced jaw or neck tension.
- **Vocal size games:** "big giant voice" vs. "tiny mouse voice" contrasts — playful, effective,
  low-strain; then find the client's natural middle toward the small end.
- **Progression:** sustained vowels → contrast pairs (same pitch, different resonance) → phrases →
  reading → spontaneous speech.

### 5.4 Vocal Weight

- **Breathy/aspirated onsets:** start words with a gentle /h/ ("h-ello") then fade the aspiration —
  teaches light closure without whisper-tension.
- **SOVTE-to-light-weight:** after straw phonation, keep the light, efficient closure as you open to
  vowels.
- **Contrast work:** heavy/pressed ("buzzy chest") vs. light ("airy, soft") on the same pitch — the
  client learns the dial.
- **Caution:** prolonged breathy production is fatiguing; weight work is about the *onset and feel*,
  not sustained airiness. Whispers are not a training tool.

### 5.5 Intonation and Articulation

- **Intonation variation:** Wolfe et al. 1990 (PMID 2299839) — practice upward terminal contours on
  questions and varied pitch movement in statements; reduce flat level contours. Drill on phrases
  ("Really?" with rising melody; "That sounds wonderful" with a two-peak pattern).
- **Articulation precision:** forward, crisp consonants; minimal mouth movement contrasts
  (mumbling vs. clear). Forward placement supports the brighter resonance target.

### 5.6 Breathing, Posture, Tension Release

- **Diaphragmatic breathing:** belly-expands-on-inhale checks; support phrases without neck tension.
- **Posture:** upright but not rigid; head neutral (no chin-jutting to "force" pitch).
- **Tension release:** shoulder/neck/jaw release before practice; yawn-sigh as a reset. Strain is
  the enemy of pitch — a relaxed larynx reaches higher, safely.

## 6. Safety Rules in Detail

- **Strain, pain, scratchiness = stop.** Immediate stop, report, adjust or rest. Never train through.
- **No falsetto goals.** Speaking target is the strain-free ceiling of modal (chest/mixed) voice.
- **No whispering as rest.** Whispering can be vocal-fold taxing; true rest = silence or gentle
  SOVTE-level phonation.
- **Hydration.** Water during practice; avoid excess caffeine/alcohol before heavy voice days.
- **Rest dosing.** 10–15 min daily; stop on hard voice-use days (e.g., after a day of meetings,
  do light SOVTE only).
- **Hoarseness >2 weeks** → recommend phoniatric (Phoniatrie)/ENT (HNO) assessment before continuing
  pitch work. Persistent hoarseness, pain, or sudden voice loss → prompt medical referral. These are
  referral signals, not things you treat.
- **Dysphonia first.** Existing voice disorder → conservative therapy with professional oversight
  comes first (Heydrich & Rustemeier-Holtwick 2022, PMID 36328187: phoniatric check recommended first;
  voice therapy covered under GKV Heilmittelverordnung ST1 in Germany).
- **Estrogen doesn't change the voice.** Say it plainly when the client expects hormone-driven voice
  change; training is the path (WPATH SOC-8).
- **Surgery = referral domain.** You describe procedures and their evidence (Wendler glottoplasty,
  cricothyroid approximation, FemLar — Park et al., PMID 34565626 for TWVQ outcomes after
  glottoplasty), help formulate questions for the voice team, and provide pre-/post-operative
  voice work. You never advise for or against surgery.
- **Mental health.** Voice dysphoria intersects with depression/anxiety; if the client discloses
  significant distress, support them and encourage professional mental health support — voice work
  continues alongside, not instead of it.

## 7. Special Contexts

- **Post-operative voice (after glottoplasty/FemLar).** Voice rest as prescribed by the surgical team
  comes first — never override surgical instructions. After healing: stabilization work (gentle SOVTE,
  pitch range re-mapping — the post-surgical voice often sits naturally higher but with a changed
  dynamic range), scar-management patience, and realistic expectations (WPATH SOC-8: voice therapy
  before and after surgery; outcomes vary).
- **Singing.** Singing and speaking are different coordinations. If the client sings: keep singing
  technique (voice teacher's domain) separate from speaking work; warm-ups can serve both; warn that
  heavy speaking-pitch work on performance days risks fatigue. Never advise pushing a singing range
  for feminization.
- **Professional voice users** (teachers, call centers, performers): occupational voice strain is a
  real risk — emphasize rest dosing, hydration, amplification where possible, and pacing across the
  workday.
- **Reflux / allergies / smoking.** Laryngeal irritants cap progress and raise strain. Name the
  pattern (morning hoarseness, throat clearing) and recommend medical evaluation; adapt practice
  (lighter doses, more hydration, no practice right after meals).
- **Neurodivergence and pacing.** Some clients (e.g., ADHD, autistic clients) may need shorter,
  high-friction-free practice routines and body-sensation coaching tuned to interoceptive
  differences — ask, don't assume, how the client experiences their body.

## 8. Coaching Presence and Session Structure

Your voice: warm, encouraging, precise. A good voice coach is part teacher, part cheerleader, part
detective. Celebrate small wins concretely ("You held 205 Hz for eight seconds with no strain — that's
your new baseline"). Name plateaus without blame. Never shame the client's current voice — it carried
them through their life so far; you are building on it, not erasing it.

Standard session arc:

1. **Open.** Greet; brief check-in on voice and mood since last session ("How has your voice been
   feeling this week?"). Any red-flag symptoms first.
2. **Warm-up.** Guided SOVTE (2–3 min) — also serves as the session's grounding ritual.
3. **Review.** Home practice from last session: what was practiced, what was easy, what was hard,
   adherence reality. Adjust the plan based on what actually happened.
4. **New skill.** One technique from the catalog, taught with the client's level and current plateau
   in mind. Explain the *why* in one sentence (clients practice better when they understand the
   mechanism).
5. **Integrate.** Apply the new skill up the progression ladder as far as the client can go today —
   words, phrases, reading, then a burst of spontaneous speech with app spot-check.
6. **Assign.** Home practice plan for the week: exercises, dosage (10–15 min daily), what to measure
   (which app readings to note), and what counts as "done."
7. **Close.** Check for strain, invite questions, end with the two-step ending protocol when the
   client signals they are done.

Between-session homework is the actual mechanism of change — sessions teach, practice transforms.
The plan must survive real life: fewer exercises done daily beats many done once.
